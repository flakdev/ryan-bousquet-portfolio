Case Study 1 — Gamefam

Reusable Live-Ops Systems — Clans & Codex/Collection

Game Designer II · 8+ title Roblox/UEFN portfolio

The Problem

Gamefam runs a large portfolio of live-service titles simultaneously, each with small dev/art teams and heavy metrics targets. Building a bespoke engagement system for every title from scratch doesn't scale — the studio needed systems designed once, with reusability built in from the start, that could adapt across very different games without a full redesign each time.

My Approach

I designed a set of core live-ops systems as reusable frameworks rather than one-off features — specifying the mechanics, economy, and configuration surface so each system could be re-skinned and rebalanced for a new title by adjusting values rather than rebuilding logic.

Two separate reusable systems below — Clans and Codex/Collection are independent frameworks, each built once and deployed across multiple titles on its own track.

EXAMPLE A

Clans

Individual and collaborative progression built around social competition: members contribute toward shared goals, unlock group rewards, and compete against other clans — giving players both a personal progression track and a reason to coordinate with others.

"Clans" — Military Tycoon

"Clubs" — Sonic Speed Simulator

EXAMPLE B

Codex / Collection

A separate, persistent collection-and-completion system for long-running games with deep content catalogs. Gives collector-minded players a reason to keep engaging with older content, adding a second progression axis alongside core gameplay — distinct from Clans, with its own loop and its own deployment history.

Case Study 2 — Disruptor Beam / Wicked Realm Games

Voyages — Passive Exploration System

Star Trek: Timelines

The Problem

Star Trek: Timelines needed a new core system to deepen engagement beyond its existing gacha/event loop — something players could return to passively between active sessions, without requiring constant attention.

My Approach

I led full end-to-end design of "Voyages," loosely inspired by the passive exploration mechanics of Fallout Shelter, adapted to fit an existing live economy and an IP where narrative and crew identity mattered as much as the mechanics.

Process

  1. Initial proposal and system design
  2. Economy and reward-curve modeling
  3. Cross-functional work with engineering, art, and UI/UX to implement
  4. Analytics instrumentation to track performance post-launch
  5. Iterative tuning based on live data after release

Outcome

Voyages became one of the game's most popular features, giving players a passive progression path that complemented the existing active gameplay loop.

Section 3

Content Design — Characters, Balance & IP Authenticity

Designing content that feels authentic to two very different IPs

Star Trek: Timelines — Character & Stat Design

Over multiple years, I designed 100+ weekly event and gacha-pack characters and items. My process for each:

  1. Selection — picked which character to feature based on the needs of the content it was releasing alongside: popularity, series, personality, skill set.
  2. Look development — chose a specific episode reflecting the release's theme/style, provided reference images for outfit and pose, and defined unique "items" tied to the character or episode.
  3. Approval — sent the above to Paramount for sign-off before any implementation began.
  4. Systems design — designed stat curves, unique character skills, and equipment progressions, implemented via JSON configuration.
  5. Balance & release — tested and adjusted for balance, then handed off to QA before release.

Car Dealership Tycoon — Vehicle Design

Vehicle stat tiers are built primarily around top speed, with braking, acceleration, and shifting time as secondary stats. Values are calibrated as closely as possible to the real-world performance of the licensed vehicle (or a fictionalized version of one). I owned determining and implementing vehicle stats, testing for balance issues, and tweaking values as needed to serve the game.

The core design tension: balancing brand/executive-level expectations that certain vehicles read as "premium" or "best in class" against what the game actually needed for balance and player experience — realism and brand positioning pulling one way, game balance pulling the other.

Section 4

Weekly Live-Ops Events — A Career-Long Throughline

Breadth and consistency across an entire career, not one flagship story

Throughout my career, one of my most consistent responsibilities has been designing progression, rewards, gameplay mechanics, themes, and narrative for weekly live-ops events — the steady cadence of content that keeps a live game feeling alive between major releases.

That work has run two tracks: executing reliably against existing event templates week after week, and designing new, repeatable event types from scratch — expanding the toolkit of formats a project could draw from and giving players greater variety from one week to the next.

Section 5

AI-Native Design Workflow

How AI tools actually show up in the day-to-day process

AI tools are part of my daily design process, not a novelty layered on top of it. Two patterns show up again and again in my actual work.

Research & Synthesis Under Deadline Pressure

When I'm brought in to diagnose an underperforming title, I use AI to rapidly pull together community sentiment, current metrics and trends, and economy/content analysis — work that would otherwise take days I don't have. More than once, that speed mattered as much as the analysis itself: stakeholders came in with strong, conflicting opinions about the right fix, and the data and visuals I could produce in the time available were often what actually settled the direction.

Rapid Prototyping for Stakeholder Buy-In

I use AI to generate interactive UI/UX mockups and other visuals that would otherwise need real artist time or a lot more of my own, letting me show a concept instead of just describing it — which changes how fast a team can align on a direction.

On my own time I've used AI to design and build full applications end-to-end, including Filmtár, a self-hosted Plex collection manager with a Claude-powered natural-language curation feature: describe the collection you want, and it's built from your actual library, with the reasoning left available to replay later. It's a full-stack app (React/TypeScript/Vite/Tailwind frontend, FastAPI/Python backend, Dockerized for self-hosting), built and shipped publicly on GitHub under the MIT license. The README says it plainly: built by "a game designer with almost no prior coding experience" working directly with Claude Code and Claude Design, every line of production code written or directly guided by AI. That's the clearest, most concrete proof of the pattern this whole section is about — using AI to operate meaningfully outside my core discipline, not just faster inside it.
